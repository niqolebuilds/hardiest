Equities. VIX near a yearly low while the Nasdaq's own vol index sits at 22.7 and emerging-market vol at 27 tells you the index is being held still by offsetting components, not by an absence of risk. Index calm plus single-stock churn is a regime, and regimes end.

Your benchmark, and why it's good news

The SG Trend Index — the benchmark for exactly the strategy you're building, run by the largest professional trend followers alive — was +7.49% year-to-date through July 2026, and its worst drawdown since 2000 is 20.61%. Read that properly: a mechanical, publishable, rules-based strategy has produced positive returns across twenty-six years, three crashes and two rate regimes, with a worst case you can know in advance. That is a working edge with a measured downside — the rarest thing in this business. Your job isn't to beat it by inventing something cleverer. It's to capture most of it, at a size you can hold, and let the two structural advantages you have over Winton — being small, and being awake in Asia — do the rest.

PART 02

What Hite actually said

Hite's philosophy has two elements, and most people only quote the second. The first is the reason to be here at all.

Element one — the markets are inefficient

Schwager's summary is unambiguous: "contrary to the opinion of many academics, Hite is firmly convinced that the markets are inefficient. This means that if you can develop a method that places the odds in your favor — and it doesn't have to be by very much — you can win." That is the premise the entire enterprise rests on. Gaps exist. Spreads exist. They can be captured by someone with a method. Element two — respect for risk — is not a counterargument to that; it is what keeps you solvent long enough to keep collecting.

The second element is four mechanical rules. The rules are the system. The ideas are why you'll be able to keep following it.

It is incredible how rich you can get by not being perfect. We are not looking for the optimum method; we are looking for the hardiest method. Larry Hite — Market Wizards, loc. 2,703

This is the sentence to build everything on. A perfect system is one fitted to a past that will not repeat — Schwager's own bracketed note on that page defines optimization as testing many variations and picking the best performer, and warns that the link between past and future performance is "often a very rough one." A hardy system is one that survives being wrong roughly six times out of ten and still compounds. You are not searching for the right answer. You are searching for the answer that doesn't break.

Principle 1 — Direction

Never trade counter to the market trend. No exceptions, and always follow the system.

Hite's friend — a brilliant arbitrageur with a palatial estate — refused one sell signal in gold, went long instead, and lost the house. His system would have made a fortune on that trade.

Principle 2 — Size

Maximum risk on any single trade is 1% of total equity.

Not 1% of the position. 1% of everything you have. This is the rule that converts "if you lose all your chips you can't bet" from a slogan into arithmetic.

Principle 3 — Diversification

Diversify to an extreme — many low-correlation systems, ~60 markets across six countries and every market group.

Note what he diversifies: not just markets, but systems, selected partly for their lack of correlation with each other.

Principle 4 — Volatility

Track volatility continuously; liquidate or suspend any market where risk/reward exceeds defined limits.

The rule almost every retail trader skips. Position size must shrink as a market gets wilder, automatically, without a decision being made.

And the two ideas

Price is the first fundamental. Hite's two favourite indicators are both price-versus-expectation, not price alone: a market that fails to move on news that should move it, and a market making a historic high. When the Iran/Iraq war hit the wires and gold could only manage $1, he read that as a market with no buyers left and sold it. That is your line — prices move first, fundamentals come second — stated as a testable rule rather than a belief.

Respect for risk is not just about trading. The line you highlighted is the one that generalises. His firm's president held a losing option position against advice, got bailed out by the market, and Hite immediately started looking for another job — because a man who walks through a minefield and survives has learned the wrong lesson. Getting away with it is the most expensive outcome available, in trading and in every other decision you'll make with money.

PART 03

Where the money actually comes from

A spread survives only when something specific stops it being closed. So don't hunt for gaps — hunt for the reason nobody has arbitraged one away. There are five such reasons, they're all checkable, and several of them are open to you precisely because you're small and in Jakarta.

  1. Mandate walls — big money is not allowed in

    The cleanest live example on the board. On 7 August 2026 the annualised basis on August bitcoin futures was 7.89%, against a two-year Treasury yield of 4.19%. Buy spot, sell the future, hold to convergence: a market-neutral position earning roughly 370bp over the risk-free rate. September was 6.25%, December 5.69%. That spread persists not because nobody has noticed but because custody rules, balance-sheet charges and mandate language keep most regulated capital out of the venue. You have no such constraint. This is the trade you are currently on the wrong side of — every funding payment you make as a long is somebody else's yield.

  2. Size floors — the opportunity is too small to be worth their time

    A fund running $500m cannot deploy meaningfully into a trade with $3m of capacity; the position wouldn't move their return and the operational cost exceeds the profit. At $10–25k, an enormous set of trades is large enough to matter to you and too small to matter to anyone who could compete it away. Smallness is a genuine structural advantage and it is the one that erodes as you succeed — so use it while you have it.

  3. Operating cost — nobody is staffing that desk

    Edges that require someone awake during the Asia session, or watching a market with poor data coverage, survive because paying a person to sit there costs more than the edge yields. You are already awake and already there. This is not a romantic advantage — it's an accounting one.

  4. Forced flow — somebody must trade at a bad price on a schedule

    Index rebalances, ETF creations, option expiries, margin liquidation cascades. These are participants transacting because they have to, not because they want to, and the price they accept is worse than fair. A dated example from this very week: the $6.4bn bitcoin options expiry on Friday, with max pain at $68,000 while spot sat near $79,000 — a large, known, calendarised flow. Forced flow is the most reliably recurring inefficiency in markets because the calendar publishes it in advance.

  5. Convexity underpricing — people systematically discount rare large moves

    This is the deepest one, and it is why trend following has worked for four decades. Human beings under-price the tails: they sell the far option too cheap, they exit the winner too early, they don't believe a market can keep going. Trend following is a machine for collecting that mispricing. And it is measurable — VIX at 15.85 sits near its 2026 low, which means the market's price for optionality is currently cheap. Cheap convexity is a condition you can wait for.

The test this gives you

Before any position, name which of the five is your reason. Why has this not been arbitraged away? If you can name it — the venue is walled off, the size is beneath them, the hour is unstaffed, the flow is forced, the tail is underpriced — you have a candidate edge worth testing. If you cannot name one, you have a hope wearing a chart. This isn't a filter designed to stop you trading. It's a search you can actually run, and it converts "find an edge" from a mystery into a checklist.

If you don't bet, you can't win. Larry Hite — Market Wizards, loc. 2,725

Which is the half of his two rules that gets forgotten, and it comes first. Read the pair the way he wrote it and the logic inverts: the 1% rule is not a brake, it's a licence. If you can only survive one mistake, you can only make one bet. At 1% risk you can be wrong ten times running and still be hunting on the eleventh — and since the eleventh is where the trend usually is, the risk limit is what buys you the winner. Hite diversified across sixty markets not to be timid but to be greedy: he wanted exposure to every trend that showed up anywhere, rather than betting his year on picking the right one in advance.

Everything from Part 06 onward is engineering in service of that. The limits exist so you can keep taking shots.

PART 04

How to read an asset class

Six questions, asked in the same order, of anything you're thinking about. The point is that they're answerable — every one resolves to a number or a yes, so a view becomes something you build rather than something you have.

  1. What regime is it in?

    Price above or below the 200-day average, and is that average rising or falling over 20 days? Two bits of information, four states. Trend systems only work in two of them. Compute it, don't eyeball it.

  2. Does holding it pay me or charge me?

    Equities pay you earnings and dividends. Treasuries pay you 4.70%. Gold pays nothing and costs storage. A long perp charges you funding — and the short side collects it. Carry is the return you get for doing nothing, and it compounds silently in both directions.

  3. Where is its volatility versus its own history?

    Not versus other assets — versus itself. A 28 on gold's vol index means something completely different from a 28 on Bitcoin's. Cheap vol relative to its own range means optionality is on sale and stops are tight; expensive vol means the opposite. This is the single most useful and least used number in retail trading.

  4. What does it correlate with that I already own?

    A new position that moves with your existing book is not a new bet, it's a bigger version of an old one. Check it against what you hold before you check anything else about it.

  5. Which of the five gaps applies?

    Mandate wall, size floor, unstaffed hour, forced flow, underpriced tail. Name one, from Part 03. This is the question that separates a trade from a guess.

  6. Only now — how much?

    Take the answer to question three into the sizing math in Part 09. Note the order: size is the last question, never the first. Conviction never enters.

PART 05

Reading crypto, and reading memecoins

Your asset class, run through the six questions — and it deserves its own part, because crypto publishes data no equity market does. Positioning, leverage and the cost of carry are all visible in real time. That's not a hazard; it's an instrument panel most asset classes don't come with.

First: it isn't one asset class

Analysing all of it with one framework is the first mistake, because the segments have genuinely different drivers:

Bitcoin — a macro asset

Driven by liquidity, rates and ETF flow. Increasingly behaves like a high-beta risk asset with a fixed supply schedule. Analysed with flow and positioning data.

Majors — platform beta

Ether and the large L1s. Correlated to BTC but with real dispersion, and driven partly by usage. Analysed as tech beta plus network activity.

Cashflow tokens

Protocols with actual fee revenue. These are the only crypto assets you can value the way you were trained to value anything at Siloam — revenue, margin, multiple.

The carry layer

Stablecoins, basis, funding, staking yield. No directional view required. This is where crypto most resembles a money market, and where the 7.89% basis lives.

Memecoins — pure reflexivity

No cashflow, no usage, no valuation anchor. The asset is attention. Analysable, but with a completely different toolkit — see below.

Why it matters

A DOGE position and a DeFi-revenue position are not both "crypto exposure." They fail for different reasons, on different days, and belong in different sleeves.

The one combination worth memorising

Price alone is ambiguous. Price together with open interest is not — and this 2×2 is the highest-value thing in crypto analysis relative to how simple it is:

Price ↑   OI ↑

New leveraged longs piling in. The move is real but the structure is fragile — this is what a liquidation cascade is built out of.

Price ↑   OI ↓

What just happened. Shorts closing, not longs opening. Leverage is leaving as price rises — a more durable advance with less fuel for a reversal.

Price ↓   OI ↑

New shorts building. Bearish in the moment, but it's also loading the spring for the squeeze that fires later.

Price ↓   OI ↓

Longs being liquidated out. Capitulation — painful, and historically where the leverage flush finishes.

What the panel says about right now — both sides

Constructive: BTC ran from roughly $62,000 to $80,500 while open interest fell to a five-month low and funding stayed under 10% annualised. That is a rally powered by shorts covering rather than fresh leverage — billions in short positions liquidated on the way up. Crypto-margined open interest is at an all-time low of 11%, meaning the collateral behind the market is mostly cash, which makes the whole structure less prone to cascade. And ETF assets went from $78.7bn to $98.6bn in a week. Cautionary: unrealised profits are high and exchange inflows are rising — coins moving to venues are usually coins about to be sold — and Friday's $6.4bn expiry has max pain down at $68,000. The level that settles it: price was rejected at the 50-week average near $81,085; the 365-day average around $83,000 is the line that would confirm a new regime. That is a testable, dated, falsifiable statement, which is what an analysis is supposed to produce.

Memecoins — the analysis, not the sermon

Start by granting the thing that's true: the inefficiency here is real. Attention is genuinely mispriced, reflexivity is a real mechanism, and a coin that goes up because it's going up is a self-reinforcing loop that can be ridden. Anyone who tells you there's no money in this is contradicted by the data. Pump.fun's profitable-wallet share rose from 30.1% in June 2025 to 73.3% by April 2026.

But look at what the same dataset says next: about 65% of those profitable traders made between $1 and $500. A lot of people winning small amounts and a few winning everything is the signature of a lottery — and the reason isn't that the attention edge is fake. It's that the edge is not "will it go up." It's two entirely different questions: am I early to attention, and can I get out at size. The second one is the binding constraint, and it's the one nobody measures.

So the analysis is a different toolkit, and it's a real one:

  1. Liquidity depth against your size

    The only question that decides whether a paper gain becomes money. Look at pool depth, then model the slippage on selling your whole position at once — not a tenth of it. If exiting your intended size moves the price more than a few percent, your real position size is whatever you can exit, not what you can buy.

  2. Holder concentration

    Top-10 wallet share. If insiders hold 40% of supply, the chart is a marketing document and you are the exit liquidity. This is checkable on-chain in about ninety seconds and it filters out most of the tail.

  3. Age, because the hazard rate falls

    97% of memecoins die and average lifespan is around a year — but that's a population average, and survival is informative. DOGE at $14.28bn is 47% of the entire $30.6bn category and is functionally a liquid large-cap. A pump.fun launch with a 0.26% graduation rate is a different asset with a different distribution. Never reason about one using statistics from the other.

  4. Attention velocity, not attention level

    The driver is the rate of change of new holders, volume and mentions — the second derivative, not the level. By the time attention is high it's already priced; the edge is entirely in being early, which means you're measuring acceleration.

  5. Float and unlocks

    What percentage of supply is actually circulating, and what unlocks when. A token with 8% float and a cliff next month is a supply event with a chart attached.

How to hold it without letting it eat the book

Ring-fence it. A separate account, funded once with an amount already mentally written off — 1–2% of net worth, not of the trading account — never topped up from the trend book. Stops don't work here (the moves gap, liquidity vanishes, and a rug isn't a price move you can be stopped out of), so the premium-style rule from Part 12 applies instead: the amount you put in is the amount you're risking, decided at entry. Sized that way it's a legitimate convex bet with a capped downside. Mixed into the trend book, it eventually eats the trend book.

PART 06

What 1% actually costs, by asset class

Principle 2 and Principle 4 combine into one calculation. Volatility sets how far away your stop must sit; the stop distance sets how much you can own. Here is that calculation run across everything you can reach, using live option-implied volatility as of 24 August 2026.

The chain is short. Annualised volatility ÷ √252 gives a typical daily move. A day's high-to-low range runs about 1.6× that daily move. A stop that isn't hit by ordinary noise sits at least two of those ranges away. And then: position size = $100 of risk ÷ stop distance. That's it — the whole of professional position sizing in one line.

What these numbers are, and what they are not

Every figure above comes from an implied volatility index — what options traders are charging today for expected movement. That is not the input a trend follower actually uses. The real input is ATR(20) computed on the instrument itself, off your own chart, looking backwards at what the market has actually done. The two differ: implied usually sits above realised, because sellers charge a premium for bearing risk. So treat this table as a planning map of relative volatility across asset classes — gold moves about twice as much as the S&P, Ether about four times — and compute the actual stop distance from your own ATR before you size anything.

The counter-intuitive result

A "safe" bond position is the largest line in your account and a Bitcoin position is one of the smallest. Risk is not what you own — it's what you own multiplied by how far it can travel before you're out.

Where leverage is actually required

Nothing here needs leverage at 1% risk. Every position is a fraction of the account. If a size you want requires margin, the honest reading is that you're taking more than 1%.

The meme coin line

At 1% risk with an honest stop, a new launch is a $166 position on a $10,000 account. If that number feels absurdly small, that is the volatility telling you the truth.

PART 07

You can't run Hite's sixty markets — and you wouldn't want to

Two separate constraints get confused here. One is about the smallest thing you can buy. The other is about how many genuinely different bets exist. Only the second one is binding for you.

Constraint one — futures have a floor, and it's above your risk budget

Hite's sixty markets were futures. Futures come in fixed contract sizes and you cannot buy a fraction of one. These are CME's micro contracts — the smallest listed size that exists:

But this constraint doesn't bind on you, because you don't trade futures. Crypto perps have no minimum contract size and fractional ETF shares have no minimum either. Bybit's floor on BTCUSDT is around 0.001 BTC — under $80. That is precisely why Part 08 is built from perps and spot ETFs rather than futures: both are divisible down to any size you like, so a $1,105 position is executable rather than theoretical. Perps carry a second advantage that matters at your timezone — they trade 24/7, so your stop is live at 3am Jakarta time, whereas a stop on SPY does nothing while New York is shut and the gap happens on the open.

Divisibility buys you sixty tickers. It cannot buy you sixty bets. What diversification pays for is uncorrelated risk, and that benefit saturates fast:

The number to keep

Translate correlation into independent bets and the alt-coin column becomes brutal. Nine genuinely diverse markets ≈ 4.1 independent bets. Sixty ≈ 6.1. But forty alt perps at ρ≈0.75 ≈ 1.3 independent bets — and nine of them is 1.29, which is to say adding thirty-one more names bought you 0.03 of a bet. Twenty alts on Bybit is not a diversified book. It is one leveraged Bitcoin position wearing twenty costumes, and it goes wrong on the same afternoon. Worse, crypto correlation isn't even stable: in calm tape it may look like 0.5–0.6, which reads as diversification, and in a drawdown it goes to 0.9+. You lose the benefit at precisely the moment you need it.

PART 08

The system, written so someone else could run it

A rule you can't hand to a stranger isn't a rule, it's a mood. Every line below is checkable at the close by someone who has never met you and has no opinion about the market.

This is a medium-term, close-only trend system on daily bars. It is deliberately slow — you look once a day, after the close, and most days you do nothing. Slowness is a feature: it removes the 15-minute chart, which is where your recorded weakness lives.

  1. Universe — nine markets, six uncorrelated groups

    Equity index SPY · Growth QQQ · EM EEM · Gold GLD · Silver SLV · Energy XLE · Rates TLT · BTC · ETH. You cannot run Hite's sixty markets, but you can run six groups that don't move together — which captures most of the diversification benefit. Never more than two open positions from one group.

  2. Regime filter — trade only with the tide

    A long is permitted only when the close is above the 200-day moving average and that average is higher than it was 20 days ago. A short (crypto only, where you have the instrument) mirrors it. This is Principle 1 made mechanical: no counter-trend trades, no exceptions.

  3. Entry — the 50-day breakout

    Enter at the next open when the daily close exceeds the highest close of the prior 50 trading days. One entry per signal. No anticipating the breakout, no entering because it "looks ready." The close either did or didn't.

  4. Initial stop — 2 × ATR(20), set before you enter

    Stop = entry price − 2 × ATR(20). It goes into the platform in the same action as the entry, never afterwards. On crypto, your existing MA28-on-4H stop is a legitimate substitute only if it sits further away than 2×ATR; if it's closer, use 2×ATR. Your logged failure mode is exiting before a structural level — a stop placed in advance is the cure, and it only works if it exists before the first counter-candle.

  5. Size — solve for risk, never for conviction

    units = (0.01 × equity) ÷ (2 × ATR20). Conviction is not an input. If the resulting position is uncomfortably small, that is the correct amount of discomfort. Round down, always.

  6. Volatility ceiling — Hite's fourth principle

    If ATR(20) ÷ price is more than 1.5× its own median over the past year, halve the position. If it's more than 2.5×, skip the market entirely until it settles. Re-check weekly and reduce open positions the same way. This is what stops a gold-in-January or an oil-at-OVX-47 from quietly becoming your largest exposure.

  7. Exit — the 20-day low, and nothing else

    Exit at the next open when the close falls below the lowest close of the prior 20 trading days, or when the 2×ATR stop is hit — whichever comes first. There is no profit target. Trend following pays for the one trade in ten that runs; a target is the mechanism by which you guarantee never taking it.

  8. Portfolio heat — 6% open, 2% per group

    Total risk across all open positions never exceeds 6% of equity. No group carries more than 2%. When a new signal would breach either cap, you skip it and log the skip. Correlation is the thing that turns six 1% risks into one 6% risk.

  9. Drawdown governor — shrink automatically

    At −10% from your equity high, risk per trade drops to 0.5%. At −15%, it drops to 0.25%. At −20%, you stop taking new entries for one full month and review the log. The governor is automatic because the moment you need it is the moment you'll least want to apply it.

  10. The override rule

    There is no override. If you take a discretionary action, it gets logged in red with the reason, and it doesn't count toward the system's track record — it counts toward yours. Hite's friend was one override away from keeping the estate.

Before this goes anywhere near money

None of these parameters are sacred — 50/20/200/2×ATR are conventional starting values, not discovered truths, and I have not backtested them for you. Run them on your own data before you fund them, and expect the honest result: something that makes money slowly with a 15–20% drawdown built in. If your test shows a smooth equity curve, you have fitted the past, which is precisely the trap Schwager flags on the page you highlighted.

What actually compounds at this account size

If the goal is compounding capital, this part changes the job description — not to make it smaller, but to put the two engines in the right order. Contributions build the capital; the edge multiplies it. Both matter, and for the next few years the first one is doing more of the work than feels intuitive.

And the crossover point is exact: at $800/month of contributions, returns only overtake contributions once the account passes $64,000 — and that assumes 15%. At SG Trend's actual 7.5%, the crossover is $128,000. Until then your salary is the engine and the market is the multiplier, not the other way round.

So the first system to build is a contribution system: automated, a fixed percentage, moved on payday before anything discretionary, and instrumented so that contribution rate sits next to returns as a headline number. It is boring next to Larry Hite. It is also, on the arithmetic above, worth more than any edge you could realistically develop in the next three years.

Frequency is a cost

Contributions don't multiply with activity — costs do. At 0.15R per round trip you lose 34% of your edge whether you trade 12 times a year or 150. Compounding wants the fewest decisions that still capture the trend.

Drawdowns are asymmetric

Lose 20%, need +25% to get flat. Lose 50%, need +100%. Lose 65% — the 1-in-20 outcome at 5% risk — and you need +186%. For a learning account that's tuition. For a compounding account it's years erased.

Perps carry badly

High cost, high variance, and negative carry — you pay funding to hold. An equity index pays you earnings growth for doing nothing. Perps are a speculation vehicle, not a compounding one.

The architecture that follows

A contribution engine feeding a boring core at 75–85% — cheap, broad, few decisions — with a satellite sleeve at 15–25% running the system in Part 08, sized so that a −20% drawdown in the sleeve is a −4% drawdown on total capital.

And then the benchmark that makes the whole thing honest, which almost no retail trader ever sets: the satellite has to beat the core, after all costs. Not beat zero — beat what that money would have done sitting in the index untouched. The ledger is what lets you actually know which, and knowing is the entire point: an edge you can prove is an edge you can scale with confidence when the capital arrives. That's the real argument for building the sleeve now while the account is small. Skill compounds faster than capital does at this size, and it's the skill that decides what the capital is worth in ten years.

One drag nobody models

You earn in IDR, convert to USD, and custody in Hong Kong and on Bybit. FX conversion spread on the way in and out, plus Indonesian tax treatment of foreign investment income, never appears in a trading P&L — and comes straight off compounding. Price both before optimising a stop-loss by a tenth of an ATR. I don't know your specific rates; find them and write them down.

PART 12

The Greeks, and which side of an option you're allowed to be on

An option's profit and loss is an itemised bill with four lines. Learn to read the bill and options risk management is almost entirely done.

Your perp has exactly one exposure: price. That's why the sizing formula in Part 08 works — exposure is fixed at whatever you set, and only price changes. An option has four exposures at once, which is how you end up right about direction and still down 39%.

Delta — the price moved

How much you make per $1 the underlying moves. A perp is delta 1.00, permanently. A 30-day at-the-money BTC call is delta 0.54 — you hold the equivalent of half a coin.

Gamma — your size changed

Delta is the speed; gamma is the acceleration. That same call is delta 0.24 if BTC drops 10%, and delta 0.80 if it rises 10%. Your position resized itself while you slept.

Theta — a day passed

Rent. That call bleeds about $72 a day if nothing happens. You pay it when long, collect it when short. Nothing else in your system charges you for the passage of time.

Vega — fear was repriced

Profit or loss per one point of implied volatility. That call is worth about $89 per vol point — so a move from 45 to 38 costs $626 with the price completely unchanged.

Gamma deserves a second look, because it is trend following in mechanical form. Price rises, your position grows; price falls, your position shrinks. That is automatic pyramiding into winners and automatic de-risking into losers, without a rule and without you being awake. Long options and trend following share a payoff shape — many small losses as premium bleeds, rare large wins — which is why Hite's line about not knowing many people who got rich taking small profits is really a statement about convexity.

BTC goes nowhere long call, 14 days flat, vol 45→38$4,183$0$0−$626−$1,007−$1,613

Black-Scholes repricing at r = 4.70%. Rows don't sum exactly to the result — the residual is what the Greeks failed to explain, and it grows with the size of the move. That is the point: the Greeks are a local approximation and they break down precisely on the days that matter, which is why desks reprice the whole book at ±10/20/30% against a range of vol shifts rather than managing on Greeks alone.

Read row one. Delta and gamma are both zero — BTC didn't move an inch. You were right about direction and still lost 39% of your stake, entirely to rent and a vol drop. Nothing in a trend system prepares you for a position that loses while the chart does nothing.

Read rows two and three together. Same eight days, same contract, opposite sides. Long, gamma out-earns delta — most of the profit came not from being long but from getting longer as it ran. Short, you collect $1,735 and lose $14,024: eight times what you were paid, with theta the only positive line on the board.

Read row four and stop. One session. You collect $59.90 an ounce and lose $341.60, and the vol spike alone — $81.30 — cost more than everything you were paid. This is the day Hite's friend loses the estate on, and it arrives without notice.

The rule this produces

Ask of each scenario: before entering, what is the most I can lose? The two you bought have an answer — $4,183 and $1,735, known at the moment of the click. The two you sold have no answer at all. So: buy options, don't sell them, and the premium you pay is your 1% risk unit. On a $10,000 account that's $100 of premium and no stop required — the loss is capped by construction, you cannot be liquidated, and a weekend gap cannot enlarge it. Gold's 30 January cannot touch you. That is a rare and valuable property, and its price is row one: you pay theta, so you can be entirely right and still lose because it happened on the wrong fortnight.

Which answers the delta-neutral question

A delta-neutral spread cancels the net deltas so the position is indifferent to small directional moves. What's left is a bet on gamma, vega and theta — on how much things move and how fear gets priced, rather than which way. Delta-neutral is not risk-neutral, and the name does real damage on that point: sell one and you are short gamma, collecting a little each quiet day and losing a great deal on the day it gaps.

The skew is the thing. Trend following is positively skewed — many small losses, rare enormous wins, worst day bounded by your stop. Short volatility is negatively skewed — many small wins, rare enormous losses, worst day bounded by nothing. Opposite temperaments, and a short-vol equity curve is beautiful and smooth right up until it isn't. Hite's arbitrageur friend is on your page precisely because a smooth curve teaches the wrong lesson about how much you can carry.

So if you want volatility exposure, take the long-vol side: a debit spread or a long straddle sized so the premium is the 1% unit. You keep the positive skew, the loss is capped at what you put in, and no margin call arrives at 3am Jakarta time.

And the last trap, from your own page

Hite's cousin bought $90,000 of Merrill options with $100,000. They expired worthless — so his loss should have been capped at $90,000, leaving him $10,000. He ended up $10,000 in debt, because he borrowed to buy more as they fell. He was holding the one instrument whose downside is bounded by construction, and he unbounded it himself. The instrument gives you the cap. Only the sizing rule keeps it.

  • Log every trade: signal date, entry, stop, size, exit, exit reason, and one line on what you felt.

  • Sunday review, 30 minutes: signals taken vs. available, overrides, heat, vol ceilings breached.
  • Count overrides. Zero for four straight weeks earns a step up to 0.5% risk. Any override resets the counter.
  • Reach 1% only after 50 logged trades with no override. Fifty trades is a sample; ten is a story.

One structural note about the skipped-signal log, since your recorded pattern is being right about direction and entry and then closing on the first counter-candle. That behaviour doesn't show up in a P&L — a small win looks fine. It only shows up when you write down, next to every early exit, where the trade eventually went. After twenty entries that column becomes impossible to argue with, and it fixes the habit faster than any amount of resolve.

It is a happy circumstance that when nature gives us true burning desires, it also gives us the means to satisfy them. Ed Seykota, quoted by Schwager — loc. 2,735

Schwager quotes that line about Larry Hite, who spent years as a scriptwriter, an actor and a rock promoter before succeeding at the one thing he actually burned for. It sits three paragraphs below the four risk principles for a reason: the risk rules aren't a constraint on the life you want, they're the thing that keeps you in the game long enough to still be there living it. Every rule in Part 08 exists to make sure that whatever happens in the market, you are still standing on the other side of it — with your capital, your nerve, and your plans for Surabaya intact.

PART 14

The equation behind all of it

Everything to this point has been one trader's craft. What follows is the discipline that craft becomes when you industrialise it — and the whole field hangs off a single equation.

IR = IC × √breadth

Your risk-adjusted return equals your skill multiplied by the square root of your number of independent bets. Grinold's Fundamental Law of Active Management.

IC is the information coefficient: the correlation between what you forecast and what actually happens. Breadth is how many genuinely independent bets you get per year. And you have already computed that second number — back in Part 07, nine uncorrelated markets gave you 4.09 independent bets while forty alt perps at ρ=0.75 gave 1.32. Run identical skill through both:

Same skill (IC = 0.05), different breadth
BookIndependent betsBreadth / yearAchievable IR
40 alt perps, monthly1.3215.80.20
9 uncorrelated markets, monthly4.0949.10.35
9 uncorrelated markets, weekly4.09212.70.73

1.76× the risk-adjusted return from breadth alone, with no improvement in skill whatsoever. Your correlation problem and your alpha problem are the same equation — and breadth is by far the cheaper term to fix. Almost everyone attacks the other one.

Calibrate on IC before you go further: a genuinely good signal runs about 0.03 to 0.05. Right roughly 52% of the time. Renaissance is not 90% accuracy; it's a tiny persistent edge at enormous breadth. Anyone offering you a signal that's usually right is selling something.

And why the pipeline has to exist: alpha decays

Part 03 said gaps exist. The other half is that gaps close — published anomalies lose a large share of their return after publication, because that is what publication does. Every filter between a backtest and a bank balance takes a cut:

Which changes the goal

An edge is a depleting asset with a half-life. So the objective was never "find the strategy" — it's to build something that produces new candidates about as fast as the old ones die. That is why what follows is nine stages of infrastructure and only one stage of ideas.

PART 16

Nine stages, nine gates

Twelve months at 8–12 hours a week, in dependency order. Each stage names one thing you build and one gate — a specific test that proves you learned it rather than read it. Don't advance on a failed gate; later stages produce confident nonsense on a broken foundation.

00

Tooling

Weeks 1–3

Not "learn Python" — learn the twenty operations that matter. Loading and aligning series on a time index, resampling, rolling windows, merge_asof for irregular timestamps, and above all shift(). Pandas, numpy, matplotlib. Nothing else yet.

01

Data hygiene — "messy data into signals"

Weeks 3–6

The unglamorous 70% of the job. Survivorship bias (your universe contains only the survivors). Point-in-time data — what was knowable then, not what the database says now. Corporate actions and adjusted prices. Timestamp alignment across venues and timezones, a real problem when you trade US equities and 24/7 crypto from Jakarta. Outliers that are real versus bad ticks.

Build

A loader with a validation suite that fails loudly: gap detection, duplicate timestamps, impossible values, timezone assertion. It should refuse to return data it doesn't trust.

Gate

Deliberately introduce lookahead into your own backtest and measure how much the Sharpe improves. Feeling that number in code you wrote inoculates you permanently.

02

Signal evaluation before backtesting

Weeks 6–12

The habit that separates research from tinkering: evaluate a signal before you ever draw an equity curve. Information coefficient, IC by forecast horizon, decile spreads, hit rate versus magnitude, turnover. An equity curve is one path; an IC distribution is the whole story.

Build

An IC-by-horizon table for one signal: 1, 5, 10, 20, 60 days. The shape of that decay curve is your holding period — you stop guessing it.

Gate

Report a signal's IC with a confidence interval. If the interval spans zero you've learned more than a backtest would have told you.

03

Factor models

Weeks 12–18

The stage that quietly kills the most retail strategies. Cross-sectional regression, risk factors versus alpha factors, residualization. The punchline is blunt: regress your strategy's returns on market, size, value, momentum, quality and volatility — whatever survives is your alpha, and it's usually far less than you hoped. Most "edges" are a factor loading you could have bought in an ETF for nine basis points.

Build

Regress your strategy's returns against a standard factor set. Report the intercept, its t-statistic, and the R² of the fit.

Gate

Alpha t-stat below 2 after factor adjustment means you don't have alpha, you have exposure. Say that about your own strategy without flinching, then decide what to do.

04

Backtesting without lying to yourself

Weeks 18–24

Walk-forward analysis. Purged cross-validation with an embargo, because financial data overlaps and ordinary k-fold leaks the future into the past. And the big one: multiple testing. Try a hundred variants and the best looks excellent by chance alone, so the Sharpe must be deflated by how many things you tried.

Build

A walk-forward harness with a genuine out-of-sample lockbox — data you may touch exactly once, at the end.

Gate

Count the variants you actually tested, then compute the deflated Sharpe. Most promising backtests don't survive this, and finding out here costs nothing.

05

Order book and microstructure

Weeks 24–30

The anatomy: price levels, depth, queue position, the spread as compensation for adverse selection. Why a market order pays the spread and a limit order pays in option value — you are writing a free option to anyone better informed. Maker/taker economics, and on your venue, funding as the perpetual's carry.

Build

Reconstruct a simple limit order book from tick data for one instrument. Watch a large order arrive and walk the levels.

Gate

Explain why a resting limit order is a short option position, and what you're paid for bearing it.

06

Market impact and capacity

Weeks 30–34

The most elegant result in the field: impact scales with roughly the square root of order size relative to daily volume, not linearly. Cost ≈ spread + k·σ·√(Q/V). So every strategy has a hard capacity ceiling — a size above which its own trading destroys the edge. This is also your single largest structural advantage right now, because you sit far below anyone's limit.

Build

A cost model for your own markets and sizes. Then recompute every backtest net of it.

Gate

State your strategy's capacity in dollars — the size at which the edge goes to zero. If you can't compute it, you don't yet know what you own.

07

Portfolio construction

Weeks 34–40

Turning signals into weights — where breadth is actually harvested. Mean-variance optimisation and why it's famously fragile (it maximises estimation error). Covariance shrinkage. Risk parity and volatility targeting. Turnover penalties, because the optimiser will happily trade you into bankruptcy through costs.

Build

Convert your signal set into weights with a volatility target and an explicit turnover penalty. Compare against equal-weight, and be honest when equal-weight wins — it often does.

Gate

Show the optimised portfolio beats naive equal-weighting out of sample, after costs. A genuinely hard bar; failing it is informative.

08

Production, and debugging in the dark

Weeks 40–52

Not "push code straight to production" — that phrase is recruiting copy. The real sequence is shadow mode (the system runs and logs every order it would have sent, trading nothing), then canary capital at 1–5% of target size, then staged scale-up with automated kill switches on drawdown, position limits and order rate. Plus reconciliation: your recorded position against the exchange's, daily, because they will diverge and the difference is always a bug.

Production debugging has one hard question — is this a bug or a regime change? The discipline that answers it is logging your expected values alongside actuals, so divergence is visible immediately rather than inferred from a bad month.

Build

Run the strategy in shadow mode for 30 days. Reconcile daily. Log expected versus actual fills and count the difference in R.

Gate

Thirty consecutive days of clean reconciliation before a single dollar goes live. This is the stage where people lose money to typos rather than to markets.

09

The alpha lifecycle

Ongoing, forever

Where the pipeline becomes a pipeline. Monitor IC live and watch it decay. Set a retirement rule in advance — the drawdown or IC threshold at which a signal switches off — and write it while you're calm, because you won't be calm when it triggers. Then feed the queue: new candidates entering at stage 01 as mature ones age out at stage 09.

Build

A monitoring dashboard: live IC by signal with its confidence band, realised versus expected costs, current portfolio heat.

Gate

Retire a signal you like, on the rule, on schedule, while it still has sentimental value. The hardest gate here, and the one that decides whether you're running a process or a hobby.

Month 3

You catch a lookahead bug in your own code before it reaches a backtest. You've stopped being impressed by equity curves.

Month 6

You kill your own favourite strategy after the factor regression. It stings, and you do it anyway.

Month 12

You can state your strategy's capacity in dollars, its IC with a confidence interval, and the rule that will retire it.

None of the nine stages need to be world-class. They need to be present, honest and hard to break — which is Hite's sentence again, applied to the process rather than the trades. A mediocre signal with rigorous validation makes money; a brilliant signal with no validation is indistinguishable from luck until the day it isn't.

~2 hrs

Introduce lookahead deliberately and measure what it buys you.

Do this

  1. Start from your clean Version B backtest and note its Sharpe.
  2. Inject three bugs, one at a time: remove the shift; rank using the next 5-day return; restrict the universe to instruments that still exist today.
  3. Record the Sharpe after each. Four numbers in one table.
  4. Write one line next to each: what real-world impossibility that bug assumed.

Passed when

You have the table, and you can never again see a Sharpe above 2 without first asking which bug produced it.

02

IC with an error bar

~2 hrs

Report a signal's information coefficient with a confidence interval.

Do this

  1. Pick one signal. For each horizon h in 1, 5, 10, 20, 60 days, compute the rank correlation between the signal at t and the return from t to t+h.
  2. Bootstrap each one — resample with replacement 10,000 times, take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. bootstrap_ci() in the stats skill does this.
  3. Put IC and its interval in a table, one row per horizon.
  4. Find the horizon where IC peaks. That is your holding period — you no longer guess it.

Passed when

The table exists and you know which horizons have intervals that cross zero. Those signals are noise, and you found out for free.

03

Alpha or exposure

~3 hrs

Alpha t-stat below 2 after factor adjustment means you have exposure, not alpha.

Do this

  1. Get a standard factor return set — the Ken French data library publishes them free for equities.
  2. Regress your strategy's daily returns on those factors.
  3. Report three numbers only: the intercept (your alpha), its t-statistic, and the R² of the factor fit.
  4. If R² is high, most of what you built was already buyable in an ETF. Name which factor.

Passed when

You say your own alpha t-stat out loud without softening it — and if it's under 2, you've written down what you'll do about it.

04

Count your attempts

~1 hr

Compute the deflated Sharpe ratio for how many variants you actually tried.

Do this

  1. Write down every variant you tested. Honestly — including the ones you abandoned after one look and the parameters you nudged.
  2. That count is almost always higher than you remember. Use the real number.
  3. Deflate your best Sharpe by it: with N trials, the best of N random strategies looks good by chance alone.
  4. Compare reported Sharpe against deflated Sharpe.

Passed when

You have both numbers and the gap between them. Most promising backtests don't survive this, and learning it here costs nothing.

05

The free option you wrote

~1 hr

Explain why a resting limit order is a short option position.

Do this

  1. Place a limit buy below the market on Bybit, small size. Leave it.
  2. Ask: under what conditions does this actually fill? Write the answer before it fills.
  3. Note that it fills exactly when someone is willing to sell to you at that price — which is when they know or believe something you don't.
  4. Now identify what you're paid for bearing that. That payment is the spread.

Passed when

You can define adverse selection in one sentence, using your own resting order as the example.

06

Your capacity, in dollars

~2 hrs

State the size at which your strategy's edge goes to zero.

Do this

  1. Take the impact law: cost ≈ half the spread + k·σ·√(Q/V), where Q is your order and V is daily volume.
  2. Estimate σ and V for one market you actually trade.
  3. Compute your expected edge per trade in the same units.
  4. Solve for the Q where cost equals edge. That number is your capacity.

Passed when

You can state one dollar figure. And you'll notice it's far above what you trade — which is the advantage you currently hold over every fund.

07

Beat equal weight

~4 hrs

Show your optimised portfolio beats naive equal-weighting out of sample, after costs.

Do this

  1. Build the boring benchmark first: equal weight across your nine markets, rebalanced monthly.
  2. Build the optimised version: volatility target plus an explicit turnover penalty.
  3. Run both on out-of-sample data only, net of the cost model from drill 06.
  4. Compare Sharpe and turnover.

Passed when

You have an honest comparison — including if equal-weight won, which happens often and is a real finding, not a failure.

08

Thirty clean days

30 days

Thirty consecutive days of clean reconciliation before a dollar goes live.

Do this

  1. Run the strategy in shadow mode: it computes and logs every order it would send, and sends nothing.
  2. Each day, log the expected fill price beside what the market actually did.
  3. Reconcile daily: your recorded position versus what the exchange says. Any divergence is a bug, not a rounding issue.
  4. Count the difference between expected and actual in R.

Passed when

Thirty consecutive days reconcile with zero unexplained differences. This is the stage where people lose money to typos rather than markets.

09

Kill something you love

Ongoing

Retire a signal on the rule, on schedule, while you still like it.

Do this

  1. Write the retirement rule now, while nothing is wrong: the IC level or drawdown at which a signal switches off.
  2. Track live IC against its confidence band, weekly.
  3. When a signal breaches the rule, turn it off that week. Not after one more month.
  4. Log what you retired and why. That log is your decay history.

Passed when

You've actually switched one off on schedule. This is the hardest gate here and the one that decides whether you're running a process or a hobby.

The rule about order

Do them in sequence and don't advance on a failed gate. Drills 03 and 04 will most likely kill a strategy you're fond of — that's them working. A pipeline that kills your bad ideas cheaply is worth more than one that finds good ones slowly.
